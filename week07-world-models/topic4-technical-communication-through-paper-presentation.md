# Week 7, Topic 4: Technical Communication Through Paper Presentation

## Topic Overview

**Plain-English summary first.** The Recorded Research Paper Presentation due at the end of this module is worth 10% of your course grade. A good paper presentation is a separate skill from understanding the paper, and it is worth taking seriously. This Topic is the practical how-to: structure that works for a 12-to-15-minute talk, slide design principles, how to pitch the technical depth to a mixed audience, what recording tools to use, common failure modes to avoid, and accessibility expectations. Most of the advice here is generic to scientific presentations; the specifics are tuned for an asynchronous-online recorded format.

### A structure that works

For a 12-to-15-minute paper presentation, the following six-section structure is reliable. Adjust the lengths to your paper’s emphasis but keep all six sections:

1.  **Title slide and the “why care” opener (1 minute)**. Paper title, authors, venue, year. One sentence on why this paper is interesting to you, in language a classmate from outside your sub-area can follow. Setup, not summary.
2.  **Problem and prior work (2 to 3 minutes)**. What problem is the paper solving? What was the state of the art before this paper, and what was wrong with it? Two or three citations are enough; you are not writing a review article.
3.  **The method (4 to 5 minutes)**. The longest section. Walk through the core technical contribution. *Show diagrams and equations.* Talk to one diagram at a time; do not put four diagrams on one slide. If you have a key equation, derive or explain each symbol.
4.  **Results (2 to 3 minutes)**. The main empirical or theoretical results. Two or three figures, not all of them. Be specific about what was compared, what the baseline was, and what improved.
5.  **Limitations and open questions (1 to 2 minutes)**. Often the most-skipped section and usually the most-valuable. What does the paper NOT do? What would have to change for the approach to scale or generalize? What followup work has been done since publication?
6.  **Relevance to the course / your project (1 minute)**. Close the loop: how does this paper connect to the model families we have studied, or to your own course project? This is the most important closing slide.

### Slide design principles

- **One idea per slide.** If a slide makes two points, split it.
- **Readable font sizes.** Body text 24pt minimum; titles 32pt. Recorded video compression makes small text disappear.
- **Diagrams over words.** If you can show it with an arrow and three boxes, do that instead of a paragraph of text.
- **Equations are fine.** Walk through them with your voice; explain every symbol. Do not flash equations and move on.
- **Cite as you go.** Bottom-of-slide citations (author, year) for every figure you reuse and every claim that is not yours.
- **Reuse the paper’s figures.** The authors’ figures are usually better than what you would build. Cite them; you are presenting their work.

### Speaking calibration for a mixed audience

Your classmates have varied backgrounds. The right depth depends on the paper:

- **Standard methods (VAE, GAN, transformer, diffusion)**: you can assume them. The class has seen them across Modules 2 through 6.
- **Sub-field-specific notation**: do NOT assume. Briefly define unusual symbols or acronyms the first time they appear.
- **Mathematical depth**: aim for “an interested classmate could follow without pausing.” If you find yourself saying “by routine manipulation we obtain,” that is often where you should slow down.
- **Pace**: ~150 words per minute is the comfortable speaking rate. 12-15 minutes is ~1800 to 2250 words of script. Speaking from notes (not reading verbatim) usually produces ~10-20% more content per minute than reading.

### Recording tools

Any of these are acceptable; pick whichever you have used before:

- **ODU Panopto**: recommended for ODU-hosted reliability. Integrates with Canvas; supports captions.
- **Zoom local recording**: most familiar; start a meeting with only you, share screen, record locally.
- **Microsoft Teams**: similar to Zoom; integrates with ODU’s M365 environment.
- **Loom**: browser-based; one-click recording with auto-uploaded link.
- **OBS Studio**: free, more control over composition; steeper learning curve.
- **QuickTime (macOS) or Xbox Game Bar (Windows)**: built-in screen recording; minimal but works.

### Common failure modes

- **Going over time.** Hard ceiling is 15 minutes; cuts beyond that lose credit. Rehearse with a timer.
- **Reading slides verbatim.** If the slide already says the words, you can move on or add something. Your voice should add information, not duplicate it.
- **No pauses.** Pauses give your audience time to read figures and equations. Plan 2-3 second pauses after each main point.
- **Audio problems.** Test the recording first. Background noise, hum, or low volume tank the experience even when the slides are great.
- **Skipping limitations.** A talk that only presents the wins reads as marketing. The limitations section is where you demonstrate critical thinking.

### Accessibility

Captions or an equivalent transcript are required. Two ways to comply:

- Use a recording tool with auto-captions (Panopto, Loom, Zoom, YouTube auto-CC); review and correct major errors.
- Provide a script as a separate PDF or text file alongside the video submission.

### Consider

- For the paper you are presenting, which of the six sections will be hardest to fit in your time budget? Plan extra rehearsal there.
- If you only had two minutes instead of 15, which two things from the paper would you include? That is your “elevator pitch” and a good opening slide.
- Pick one figure from the paper that confused you the first time you read it. How will you explain it differently in your talk to spare your audience the same confusion?
